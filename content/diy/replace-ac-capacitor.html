THIS PART CAN SHOCK YOU WITH THE POWER OFF

A capacitor stores electricity. It can hold a dangerous charge even after the breaker is off. Always kill the power and discharge it before touching the terminals. If any step here makes you uneasy, stop and call us.

Signs of a bad capacitor

  • Outdoor unit hums or buzzes, but the fan doesn't spin
  • The fan spins if nudged with a stick. Never with your hand.
  • Compressor clicks, hums, or won't start, and the breaker may trip
  • The top of the capacitor is bulging, or it's leaking oil
  • System works in the cool morning, then quits in afternoon heat

What you need

  • The replacement capacitor. Send us a photo of the old one's label and we'll match it.
  • Multimeter with a capacitance (µF) setting
  • Insulated screwdrivers and a nut driver
  • A capacitor discharge tool, or a 20,000-ohm 5-watt resistor with insulated leads
  • Phone camera, and masking tape for labeling wires

Read the label first

Capacitors are rated in microfarads (µF or MFD) and volts. Most home AC units use a dual capacitor with three terminals:

  • C: common
  • HERM: the compressor (hermetic) side, the larger µF number
  • FAN: the outdoor fan side, the smaller number

A label reading 45+5 µF, 440 VAC means 45 µF for the compressor and 5 µF for the fan. Your replacement must:

  • Match the µF ratings exactly, within the tolerance printed on it, usually ±6%
  • Match or exceed the voltage: a 440V part can replace a 370V part, but never the other way around
  • Fit the bracket. Round or oval doesn't matter electrically.

Steps

  1. Turn the thermostat off, switch off the AC breaker, and pull the outdoor disconnect.
  2. Remove the electrical access panel on the outdoor unit, usually a few screws near where the wires enter.
  3. Verify the power is off with your multimeter on AC volts across the incoming wires at the contactor. You should read zero.
  4. Discharge the capacitor. With the discharge tool or resistor (holding it only by the insulated leads), bridge HERM to C, then FAN to C, for a few seconds each. Then check with your meter on DC volts: it should read zero.
  5. Photograph the wiring, then label each wire with tape (C, HERM, FAN).
  6. Test the old one (optional). Disconnect it and set your meter to µF. If a reading falls more than about 6% below the label, it's bad.
  7. Move the wires one at a time from the old capacitor to the same terminals on the new one. Push the connectors on firmly.
  8. Mount the new capacitor in the bracket and tighten the strap. Make sure no wire touches a sharp edge or the fan.
  9. Close it up and restore power: panel on, disconnect in, breaker on, thermostat to Cool. Within a few minutes, both the fan and the compressor should be running.
WHY THE SHORTED-SCREWDRIVER TRICK ISN'T HERE

You'll see people discharge capacitors by bridging the terminals with a screwdriver. It works, but it throws a spark and can pit the terminals. A resistor or discharge tool does the same thing safely.

CALL A PRO IF
  • Wires are melted, scorched, or brittle
  • The contactor's contacts are pitted or burned (see replacing a contactor)
  • The fan runs on the new capacitor but the compressor still won't start. It may need a hard-start kit, or the compressor itself may be failing.
  • The breaker trips after the swap
